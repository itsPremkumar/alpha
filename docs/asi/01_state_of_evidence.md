# State of the Evidence: RSI, Takeoff, and ASI

Collected 2026-09-26. Every number carries a source in `SOURCES.md`.

## 0. Definitions, because the term is used loosely

"Recursive self-improvement" (RSI) is used inconsistently across the literature. The distinction that
matters operationally is **bounded** versus **open-ended** RSI.

| Kind | What changes | External anchor | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bounded RSI** | The system improves its *performance* at a fixed task. | The task, and its scorer. | **Demonstrated.** This is ordinary ML. |
| **Open-ended RSI** | The system modifies *itself* — its code, its architecture, or the machinery and criteria of improvement itself. | **None fixed.** | **Not demonstrated.** |

Source: arXiv 2607.07663, "Recursive Self-Improvement in AI: From Bounded to Open-ended."

Almost all impressive results described as "RSI" in 2026 press coverage are **bounded** RSI: an agent
improving a training script, a kernel, or a prompt against a fixed scorer. That is real and valuable. It is
not an intelligence explosion, and the framing matters because bounded RSI has an off-ramp — you can stop
it — while open-ended RSI does not.

## 1. The one measurement that matters: task-length time horizon

**MEASURED.** METR's metric: the duration of task a model can complete with 50% reliability, on a basket of
real software tasks with human expert baselines.

| Date | Model | Time horizon |
|---|---|---|
| 2024-03 | Claude Opus 3 | ~4 minutes |
| 2025-03 | Claude Sonnet 3.7 | ~1.5 hours |
| 2026-03 | Claude Opus 4.6 | ~12 hours |
| 2026 (latest) | Claude Mythos Preview | "at least" 16 hours — upper end of what METR can measure without new tasks |

**The doubling time accelerated from ~7 months to ~4 months.** The 7-month figure was METR's original
published trend (2019 onward). Anthropic's September 2026 update states the acceleration explicitly.

If the ~4-month doubling held, multi-week tasks would be in range in 2027. Anthropic's own framing:
*"tasks that take a skilled person days could come into range this year. In 2027, AI systems could be capable
of tasks that take a person weeks."*

**Caveats that matter and are usually dropped:**

- The 50% reliability figure understates; the 80% horizon follows a similar trendline but is lower.
- METR's own work on developer productivity estimates (arXiv 2507.09089) shows self-reported uplift is
  systematically **overestimated**. Apply that correction to every productivity anecdote.
- Task-length extrapolation across a capability regime change is not a straight line. A capability that
  requires 10,000-hour human context does not become reachable by extending hours.

## 2. Benchmark saturation as corroboration

**MEASURED.**

- **SWE-bench**: low single digits → saturated in roughly two years.
- **CORE-Bench** (reproducing published research, a prerequisite for doing original research): ~20% success
  in 2024 → saturated 15 months later.

Note the direction: CORE-Bench is the more research-relevant benchmark, and it saturated *faster*.

## 3. What is actually happening inside a frontier lab

**VENDOR-CLAIMED**, from Anthropic Institute, "When AI builds itself," updated 2026-09-18. These are
Anthropic reporting on itself. Treat as directional. They are, notably, unusually well-caveated.

| Claim | Figure | Anthropic's own caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Merged code authored by Claude | >80% (May 2026), from low single digits pre-Feb-2025 | Attribution pipeline has gaps; un-attributed lines include autogenerated code |
| Code per engineer per day | 8× vs 2024, Q2 2026 | *"almost certainly an overstatement of the true productivity gain"* |
| Employee self-reported output uplift | ~4× median, n=130, March 2026 | *"we expect the true degree of uplift … to be somewhat lower"* |
| Kernel-optimisation speedup | ~3× (Opus 4, May 2025) → **~52×** (Mythos Preview, Apr 2026) | Not a real-world training speedup; a skilled human needs 4-8h to reach 4× |

The 8× figure is the most-cited and the least reliable. The 3× → 52× figure is the most decision-relevant,
because it is a **like-for-like controlled comparison against a human baseline**.

## 4. The actual frontier: execution vs direction-setting

This is the most important section in this document, and the part most commentary gets wrong.

**AI is already superhuman at executing a well-specified experiment.** 52× versus a human's 4×, in under a
year (MEASURED within VENDOR-CLAIMED reporting).

**AI is measurably improving at picking the next step.** Given only the work *before* a research session
went off-course, and asked what to do next, with a separate model that saw how the session turned out acting
as judge:

| Date | Model | Beat the human's choice |
|---|---|---|
| 2025-11 | Opus 4.5 | 51% |
| 2026-04 | Mythos Preview | **64%** |

Bias check Anthropic ran: on 127 moments where the human's choice was *already strong*, models were judged
better only ~20% of the time. So the 64% is not "the model is better than the human" — it is "the model
extracts value from moments where the human had room to improve," which is the operationally useful claim.

**AI is not yet reliable at deciding which problems are worth solving.** Anthropic states this as the
remaining gap: *"large performance gaps persist when it comes to Claude exercising judgement in choosing
goals."*

**The strongest single demonstration** (VENDOR-CLAIMED, alignment.anthropic.com 2026-04): the automated
weak-to-strong-supervision researcher. Agents were given an open AI-safety problem, proposed hypotheses,
tested them, shared findings with parallel agents, and iterated.

- Two human researchers, ~1 week: recovered **~23%** of the achievable gap.
- Agents, 800 cumulative hours, ~$18,000 compute: recovered **97%**.
- Humans chose the problem and wrote the scoring rubric. *"Direction-setting was the only meaningful role a
  human played."*

Anthropic's own caveats: it did not transfer cleanly to production-scale models, and humans still chose
the problem and created the rubric. Both caveats are load-bearing.

## 5. Amdahl's law is already binding

**MEASURED, from Anthropic's own experience.** Two specific bottlenecks have already appeared:

1. **Human code review.** Once AI-authored and human-authored code reach parity, humans stop writing and
   only review — and if humans cannot review as fast as Claude generates, review becomes the constraint.
   Anthropic has already hit this.
2. **Organisational idea throughput.** *"an explosion of new ideas, initiatives, tools, and simulations, as a
   result of Anthropic employees working with highly capable models — far more than we have the capacity to
   pursue."*

> *"The rate at which organizations can spot and fix these bottlenecks may be a skill that improves over
> time, and it may become the most important skill for any organization."*

That sentence is the most useful thing in this document for anyone building an agent platform. The
bottleneck moves to bottleneck-detection, and that is a *system* capability, not a model capability.

## 6. The counter-argument, stated properly

**SPECULATIVE but not dismissive.** Two independent lines:

- Anthropic's own Scenario 1: these trajectories may be S-curves, not exponentials. Research taste may be a
  capability that *cannot* be obtained by scaling training inputs — requiring an architectural idea on the
  order of the Transformer. A supply-chain constraint (energy, chips, interconnect) could bind instead.
- Independent analysis (pelles.ai, and a widely-circulated critique of a 2026 RSI paper) argues the
  measured evidence shows **no sign of fast takeoff** — that the intelligence explosion fizzles rather than
  compounding.

The honest summary: **bottlenecks have moved three times** (write code → run experiments → choose what to
work on), and each move produced a large measured gain before the next bottleneck bound. That is
consistent with both "compounding acceleration" and "S-curve that bends." The disagreement is about whether
direction-setting yields to scaling or requires a new idea. Nobody knows.

## 7. Why this matters for a project like alpha

Three transferable conclusions, none of which require believing a takeoff scenario:

1. **Build for the bottleneck moving.** Every architecture that hard-codes "the model does the work" will
   be wrong within a year. What survives is the system that can absorb a capability jump without redesign.
2. **Orchestration is where the headroom is** — see `02_learned_orchestration.md`. Sakana Fugu beats every
   model in its own pool by routing well. No new model required.
3. **The scarce skill is judgement, and judgement is a systems problem.** Which agent, which task, which
   experiment is worth running, whether a result is trustworthy. That is dispatch, evaluation and
   verification — not generation. alpha's weakest area is exactly this, and it is the area that matters
   most. See `04_alpha_gap_analysis.md`.

## 8. Coordination is harder than it looks

Anthropic argues for the *option* of a verifiable slowdown, and makes a specific technical point worth
recording:

> *"Due to the unique characteristics of AI systems, the detectability (a lower standard than
> verifiability) element of this arms control problem is much more challenging than with other
> technologies. Training runs are far easier to conceal than missile silos, their inputs are general-purpose,
> and the incentive to defect quietly is enormous."*

General-purpose inputs mean a training run cannot be distinguished from ordinary compute. Any monitoring
scheme that assumes otherwise is broken by construction. This is a useful corrective to optimistic
forecasting, and it also means on-device attestation and provenance recording are the only durable
verification primitive — which is why `01` and the OpenClaw work in `03` both land on provenance.
